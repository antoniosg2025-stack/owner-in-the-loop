Owner in the loop: learning a business owner's reward when he is only sometimes watching

My vending robots, a vision-based store and a team of LLM agents run seven small businesses. Each needs my approval, and I am rarely available. How can these agents learn my reward from ratings and corrections, measure how aligned they are with me, and decide what to do while I am not watching?

Why it matters · productivity

What if the agents never stopped to wait for me?

Today every agent stops at my approval, and I can only look at one business at a time. When I spent a week on one project, the others stood still. An agent that decides like me only asks when it is unsure, so the same few minutes of my attention clear many more decisions.

The robot in the field · where it goes next

A vending machine is already a robot. What it lacks is judgement.

It senses (payment, temperature, door, motors), it acts (it dispenses), and it reports every sale. Today all of its decisions still come from me: what to stock, where to go, what to drop. My four machines are the smallest real robots I own, and the place where an owner's preferences meet the physical world every week. The next step is not a better machine. It is a machine that learns how its owner decides.

Today

Reports every sale

The card reader sends telemetry on each sale. An agent drafts the restock list and I correct it before every visit. Every correction is logged.

Next

Sees its own shelf

A camera inside reads what is left, with the same vision as the GrabGo store. The machine knows its stock without waiting for a visit, and learns each location's demand and my product rules.

Then

Asks only when unsure

Reversible moves, like swapping a slow product, it makes alone. A new product, a price change or leaving a location, it asks. My weekly visit is the monitor: feedback exists only when I am there.

Later

A fleet that learns from one owner

What one machine learns from me transfers to new machines and new locations, and the same loop extends from snack machines to smart fridges, micro-stores and, one day, service robots in clinics.

Experiment 1 · building on R4 and TAC

Ratings or preferences: which teaches an agent the owner's reward?

The owner reviews spending proposals. With a preference, he picks the better of two proposals. With a rating, he labels one proposal bad, neutral or good. Both cost him one answer. I fit a linear reward from each kind of feedback (a ranking-MSE loss in the spirit of R4 for ratings, Bradley–Terry for preferences) and score it two ways: alignment with the owner (Kendall τ-b, as in TAC) and the share of proposals the agent would approve or reject differently from him.

Alignment 0.86 · decides differently 19.1%

Alignment = Kendall τ-b between the owner's true utility and the learned reward on 600 new proposals (1 = same ranking). 20 runs per point.

Share of new proposals where the agent's approve/reject differs from the owner's. This owner approves about a third of proposals.

1

Both kinds of feedback learn the owner's ranking well; preferences catch up with ratings after about 160 answers. But preferences never tell the agent where the owner's bar is: even with 160 answers the agent still approves or rejects about 19% of proposals differently from him, against about 5% with ratings. For an agent that must decide whether to act, ordinal ratings carry the information that matters. This supports R4's motivation in a non-robotic, money-valued domain.

Experiment 2 · the owner as a monitor

What should the agent do while the owner is not watching?

Decisions arrive every hour, because vending robots and an online store never close. The owner is available a few hours a week. Only then does he review the queue (up to six items an hour) and rate them; the agent refits its reward every week from the ratings it has seen. Three policies: always wait for him, always act alone, or act alone only when the learned reward is confident and the action is reversible.

When the owner is the monitor, waiting is the dominant cost, not error. Acting alone on everything makes about 7 to 13 mistakes per 100 decisions. Acting alone only on confident, reversible decisions (about a quarter of them) keeps mistakes near 0.3 per 100 and, at 20 owner-hours a week, cuts the average wait from about 290 to 65 hours. Choosing which decisions to keep for the owner, and when to ask him, is exactly the open question of learning under a monitor.

Proposed MSc thesis

Learning an owner's reward from ratings and corrections under a monitored, misaligned-teacher setting

  1. Rating-based reward learning on a deployed system. Does R4-style learning from ordinal ratings, plus corrections with reasons, reach the owner's decision bar with fewer owner answers than preferences, on vending, store and LLM-agent decisions?
  2. The owner as a monitor. Framing the owner's schedule as the monitor of a Mon-MDP, which decisions should the agent act on alone, which should it queue, and when should it ask?
  3. Alignment that drifts and teachers that disagree. Using TAC over months, how fast does alignment decay when the owner's situation changes, and how should an agent weigh a specifier's ratings against the owner's?

Case 01 · Agents · the framework

One owner, many types of decision, and a specifier in between

My businesses run on LLM agents and a few people. When I gave the agent rules, it followed them with me. When another person used the system, some rules held and others broke. The real object was never the rules. It is how I decide, which depends on my situation and changes with the type of decision.

The same owner weighs the same facts differently

Each row is a type of decision. Each column is a fact about it. Cash is tight, so the amount weighs heavily against spending. When the owner's life changes, the agent has to follow

On day 60 the owner pays off his debts, and spending stops being so sensitive to the amount. Agents that learned the old owner start asking again. The one that forgets old decisions faster keeps its errors lowest, and pays for it in questions.

A specifier answers for the owner. Whose profile does the agent learn?

From day 60, a specifier answers half of the requests. Her situation is different: more relaxed about money and brand risk. If the agent learns from whoever approves, it slowly becomes her. If her answer is kept as a comment, and the owner is asked only when they disagree, it stays the owner's agent.

Findings

1

One profile per type of decision makes about a third of the errors of a single profile (0.3 vs 1.1 per 100) and asks half as often (27 vs 57 per 100). The single profile fails where the owner's situation weighs most: spending and deploying.

2

An owner who changes has a price. The agent that forgets old decisions keeps errors lowest after the change (0.1 per 100) but asks more (36 vs 27 per 100).

3

If the agent learns from whoever approves, the specifier's profile silently replaces the owner's: 8.5 vs 1.1 wrong decisions per 100. The rules were never lost. The source of the labels changed.

Method, assumptions and limits

Each decision has a type k (sell, spend, publish, client message, deploy) and a context x: amount, reversibility, novelty, brand risk, urgency and quality of evidence. The owner approves when

owner(x, k) = 1[ wk · x + bk + ε > 0 ], ε ~ N(0, 0.35)

where wk, bk is his hidden profile for that type and ε is his own inconsistency. The agent fits logistic models online, refits every day, acts when max(p, 1 − p) is above the threshold, and asks otherwise. Every answer, and every later correction of a wrong action, becomes a training label. Forgetting uses a 15-day half-life on sample weights.

Limits: a linear owner, synthetic decisions, a fixed threshold for asking (a learned rejector is the next step), and an owner who is always available to answer.

The owner's rule is simple: pay off every card first, keep a stable cushion, and put at risk only what a fixed deposit would pay in a year. A correction with a reason teaches more than many approvals

Case 04 · SEO

Autonomy for reversible changes, and rules that travel across sites

360 → 126
questions to the owner in 12 weeks

Built on the SEO agent's real decision log: 7 of 10 documents were done alone, while 3 of 5 staging changes and 2 of 3 production changes waited for the owner. Letting only reversible changes run alone cuts the questions. A correction learned on one site travels to all of them, with one question about its scope, so repeated rules fall from 70 to 6 to 0. Treating every correction as global would wrongly block about 21 changes.

Case 05 · Vending

The owner's limits beat the textbook optimum

Restocking vending machines looks like a forecasting problem. The textbook plan ignored the owner's time and driving distance. Case 06 · GrabGo

Cheaper checkout by changing the shelf, not the camera

GrabGo turns any space into a store that charges from images: the shelf camera, or a photo the customer sends by WhatsApp. Giving look-alike products the same price cuts the money lost to recognition errors by about 90% with no new hardware.
